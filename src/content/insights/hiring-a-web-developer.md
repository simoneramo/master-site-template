Finding a good web developer can feel overwhelming. There are thousands of freelancers, agencies, and platforms competing for your business — and the quality varies enormously.

This guide will help you ask the right questions and spot red flags before you sign a contract.

## Start With the Basics

Before you look at portfolios or pricing, get clear on what you actually need:

- **What is the primary goal of your website?** (Generate leads? Sell products? Build credibility?)
- **Who is your target audience?**
- **What pages do you need?**
- **Do you need ongoing maintenance or is this a one-off project?**
- **What is your budget range?**

Having clear answers to these questions will help you evaluate whether a developer is actually the right fit.

## What to Look for in a Portfolio

A portfolio tells you a lot — but only if you know what to look at.

### Check the actual live sites

Don't just look at screenshots. Visit the actual websites. Ask yourself:

- Does it load quickly?
- Does it look good on mobile?
- Is it easy to navigate?
- Does it feel professional?

You can test speed at [pagespeed.web.dev](https://pagespeed.web.dev/).

### Look for variety

A developer who has only built sites in one industry may not understand your needs. Look for variety in sector, style, and complexity.

### Ask about their role

"Designed and developed by..." can mean many things. Did they do everything, or were they one part of a larger team? There's nothing wrong with either, but you want to know.

## Questions to Ask Before You Hire

These questions will quickly separate experienced professionals from beginners:

**About the project:**

1. Can you walk me through a recent project similar to mine?
2. What CMS will you use, and why?
3. Will I own the website and all its code?
4. What happens if I want changes after launch?

**About process:**

1. How will you communicate with me during the project?
2. What do you need from me to get started?
3. How do you handle scope changes?
4. Can you give me a fixed price, or is it hourly?

**About technical quality:**

1. How do you approach mobile responsiveness?
2. What do you do to optimise page speed?
3. How do you handle website security?
4. What is your approach to SEO?

If they can't give confident answers to most of these, keep looking.

## Red Flags to Watch For

### 🚩 No clear contract or scope of work

A handshake deal or a vague email chain is not enough. You need a written agreement that specifies what you're getting, when you're getting it, and what it costs.

### 🚩 They won't give you ownership of the code

Some developers use proprietary systems or retain code ownership. This gives them leverage over you forever. Always insist on full ownership.

### 🚩 They can't explain their pricing

"It depends" is fine as an initial answer. But if they can't give you a clear breakdown after a discovery conversation, that's a problem.

### 🚩 They disappear after launch

Ask specifically: what happens after the site goes live? If the answer is vague or non-existent, expect to struggle getting support.

### 🚩 No questions about your business

A good developer asks a lot of questions before quoting. If they jump straight to a price without understanding your goals, they're guessing.

## Freelancer vs Agency vs Boutique Studio

| Option | Pros | Cons |
| ------ | ---- | ---- |
| Freelancer | Lower cost, direct communication | Availability risk, limited capacity |
| Large Agency | Full team, broad capabilities | Expensive, you may not get senior attention |
| Boutique Studio | Senior expertise, personal service | Smaller team, limited capacity |

The right choice depends on your budget, timeline, and how much hand-holding you need.

## What Good Value Looks Like

Price alone should never be your deciding factor. The cheapest option almost always costs more in the long run through:

- Redesigns required sooner
- Security issues from poor code
- Lost revenue from slow websites
- Time spent managing a difficult supplier

Good value means a developer who:

- Delivers what was promised on time
- Communicates clearly throughout
- Builds something that performs well and looks great
- Is still available when you need them after launch

## The Bottom Line

Hiring the wrong developer is expensive. Hiring the right one is an investment that pays off for years.

Take your time with the selection process. Ask questions. Check references. Read the contract. And trust your gut — if something feels off in the sales process, it will probably get worse once the money changes hands.
